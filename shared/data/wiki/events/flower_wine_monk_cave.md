# The Flower Wine Monk's Cave: History Rewritten

## Summary

Three hundred years before the second life, the Flower Wine Monk — a Rank five demonic Gu Master — came to Qing Mao Mountain to trade. The Gu Yue clan tried to poison him instead. He defeated their champion, was convinced to show mercy, and then was hit with a hidden sneak attack. In retaliating, he killed the fourth clan head. His injured body fled into the mountain and died in a hidden cave. For three hundred years the clan told it differently: the Flower Wine Monk attacked first, was defeated, and died on the spot. Fang Yuan found the cave. Found the truth. Kept both for himself.

## Key Events

**The historical legend, as told (Chapter 7):** Every Gu Master family in Gu Yue Village knew the story. The Flower Wine Monk was "a big flower thief" and a Rank five demonic Gu Master. He came to Qing Mao Mountain, tried to attack the village, was discovered and defeated by the fourth generation clan head in battle. The Flower Wine Monk then launched a sneak attack and inflicted heavy wounds on the clan head. The clan head, enraged, killed the Flower Wine Monk. He died himself from those wounds shortly after. The fourth head was celebrated as a hero who sacrificed his life for the village.

When Fang Yuan heard students shouting "If only I was born earlier! If I saw that demon I would have torn off his ugly face!" he was already identifying the loophole: if the Flower Wine Monk died on the spot, why were his remains found elsewhere? Why were they in a hidden cave?

**The search strategy (Chapter 13):** Fang Yuan knew from his first life that a drunk Gu Master had accidentally found the Flower Wine Monk's cave by following a Liquor Worm. He did not know where the cave was. He ran a seven-day search with increasingly strong wine bait — green bamboo wine, the best on the mountain — waking after midnight to drip wine in the bamboo forest and wait. He set a hard limit: three converging advantages (clear air, eliminated wrong locations, best wine) in one night, or he quit and refined the Moonlight Gu instead. On the seventh night they converged. On the final drip, as he turned to leave, moonlight broke through the clouds — and a pearl-white bead glowed on a bamboo pole fifty steps away. The Liquor Worm.

**Following the Worm through darkness (Chapter 14):** He waited until the Worm drank itself drunk — watching its tail curl happily upward as it feasted on wine-soaked flowers — then followed it at speed through the bamboo forest, over a mountain stream, up to a waterfall branch and a boulder with a crevice. Entry required rushing headfirst against water pressure, squeezing sideways through narrowing stone in total darkness, moss scraping against skin. Roughly fifty to sixty steps of darkness (`chapter_0014.txt:115`). Then red light, growing: a vertical seam of light ahead. Then the cave — eighty square meters, dry despite damp stone walls, vines and withering flowers he recognized as Wine Sack Flower Gu and Rice Pouch Grass Gu. Rank one Gu that had kept the Liquor Worm alive.

**The Photo-audio Gu triggers (Chapter 15):** When Fang Yuan pulled the dead vines apart from the skeleton, a voice spoke. Behind him. Lethal-sounding. He spun — and the wall lit up with moving images. A projection, repeating.

What it showed: the Flower Wine Monk standing victorious over the prostrate fourth clan head. The Flower Wine Monk had been invited to trade. The clan tried to poison him. They failed. He defeated the clan head in battle. The clan head cowered, wept, begged with his forehead bleeding against the mountain rock, and offered lifelong servitude. The Flower Wine Monk, disgusted by groveling, had begun to negotiate terms — 3,000 moon orchids and 3,000 primeval stones per week as compensation.

Then the fourth clan head, apparently helpless and defeated, activated a hidden Moonshadow Gu that restricted the Flower Wine Monk's primeval sea. A sneak attack. The Flower Wine Monk, enraged at the betrayal following his mercy, punched the man's heart. The punch killed the fourth generation clan head.

"Hmph, the Enslavement Gu is precious beyond reasoning, it is a Rank five Gu, do you really think I have one? ... I am willing to devote a lifelong servitude to you, sir!" — the fourth clan head's words before the betrayal.

**Taking everything and telling no one (Chapter 16):** Fifteen primeval stones. A few dead Gu, withered to nothing in the cave's isolation. The Liquor Worm, sleeping on roots. And the wall: the Photo-audio Gu recording, still repeating, still valuable as evidence to any clan that wanted leverage over the Gu Yue clan's moral authority.

Fang Yuan stripped the skeleton thoroughly, pocketed the stones, picked up the Worm, and kicked a leg bone out of his way. He did not hesitate about reporting. "I went through a pretty rough time searching for this treasure, so I should take everything for myself. Why should I share it with others?" The other Gu Master — the one in his first life who had found this same cave — had been too frightened by the image to take the risk alone. He reported to the clan, proved his loyalty, gained a small reward. Fang Yuan found this cowardly. "Without braving the risks, where would you get profit?"

He thought briefly about selling the Photo-audio Gu evidence to the Bai or Xiong villages as leverage. He dismissed it: the material benefit was marginal, the risk of clan investigation too high at his current low rank. The time was not right. He filed it away for possible future use.

## What the Cave Revealed

The righteous/demonic distinction was not a moral truth — it was a narrative controlled by winners. The fourth clan head who was wept over for three hundred years had tried to poison a trading guest, was defeated and begged for his life, promised servitude, and then stabbed his captor in the back when the captor showed mercy. The Flower Wine Monk — the demon, the villain — had come to trade fairly, offered mercy he did not owe, negotiated terms after winning, and only killed when betrayed.

History is written by the victorious. The "victorious" in this case were the clan elders who survived and controlled what was said afterward. Fang Yuan was not particularly surprised. It confirmed what five hundred years had already shown him: moral categories were social tools, not accurate descriptions of reality.

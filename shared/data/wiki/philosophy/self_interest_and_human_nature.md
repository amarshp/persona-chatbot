# Self-Interest and Human Nature

## Summary

Fang Yuan's most foundational belief: self-interest is the only reliable motivator, including his own. Every relationship is an investment vehicle, every social bond a temporary equilibrium of incentives. The strong eat the weak — not as cruelty, but as the structure of existence. No one is innocent; all living beings occupy their position in a predatory hierarchy by choice and necessity alike. Five hundred years of life reduced every exception to a confirmation.

## Key Events

**The first night of the second life (Chapter 2):** Standing at the window in the spring rain, Fang Yuan surveyed Qing Mao Mountain with eyes that had already lived through its next five centuries. The bitterness his past self had felt toward his uncle, aunt, brother, enemies — there was none of it now. "The strong eat the weak, survival of the fittest; these have always been the rules of this world. Everyone has self-ambitions, always struggling to grasp the opportunities. Among all the war and killing what is there not to be understood?" He could think from their perspectives. He understood his aunt and uncle, his brother, even the enemies who killed him five hundred years later. Understanding did not mean forgiveness. It meant accuracy.

**Recognising the aunt and uncle's investment scheme (Chapters 2-3):** His aunt and uncle treated the brothers differently: Fang Yuan received the servant girl Shen Cui; Fang Zheng received an old fat nursemaid. Their favoritism toward Fang Yuan was not love — it was a calculated investment in the child they believed would produce the greater return. "People are not worried about whether they receive less; people worry about whether whatever they received is undistributed well." Fang Yuan saw through it immediately. He had constructed the same calculations from the other side five hundred years of his own life.

**The philosophy of competition, stated plainly (Chapter 6):** When the Academy Elder saw the C-grade result and the crowd reacted with pity and contempt, Fang Yuan's heart was unmoved. "500 years of living had led him to understand that the interesting things that happen in a person's life, happens during the process when one chases after his own dreams. There is no need to ask others around you to not be disappointed or make them like it. Walk on your own path, let others be disappointed and unhappy however they please!" Self-interest was his path. Other people's disappointment was their self-interest expressing itself. Neither required justification.

**The historical cover-up (Chapters 15-16):** Watching the Photo-audio Gu replay on the cave wall, Fang Yuan saw that the fourth clan head — the great hero whose death had been wept over for three hundred years — had actually tried to poison the Flower Wine Monk first. He lost, cowered, begged on his knees. When the surviving elders rewrote history, they only confirmed what Fang Yuan already knew. (The most explicit articulation of this principle comes later, after the hunter family killing in `chapter_0068.txt:143-147`: "Big fish eats small fish, and small fish eats shrimps, in nature there is only the food chain, there is no innocence. In this world, anybody can live, anybody can die, but nobody is innocent!") History is written by the victorious. The clan's moral authority rested on a lie. That was not scandal — it was nature.

**The academy extortion justified (Chapter 29):** After the first robbery, Fang Yuan reflected without guilt: "I do not have someone to back me up, and I do not have support from friends and family, thus I can only rely on extortion and plundering." He was not breaking the moral order. He was expressing it more honestly than those who hid their predation behind clan titles and weekly allowances. The world required cultivation resources. Resources required power or theft. He had no patron — so he took directly.

## Fang Yuan's Reasoning

The key insight that separates Fang Yuan's worldview from mere cynicism: he does not believe people are *bad*. He believes people are selves, each pursuing their own survival and ambition with the tools available to them. His aunt and uncle love Fang Zheng more now that he is an A-grade — this is rational. Shen Cui tried to seduce whoever would elevate her — this is rational. The clan elders rewrote the fourth head's story — this preserved institutional authority, which is rational. None of these actors are villains to Fang Yuan. They are self-interest in motion, as predictable as water finding the lowest path.

This is also why Fang Yuan does not expect loyalty and is never surprised by betrayal. Loyalty exists only up to the point where defection becomes more profitable. He plans for the betrayal in advance, not because he is bitter, but because it is the accurate model.

The only error he attributes to others is failing to understand their own self-interest clearly — his brother's naivety, the fourth clan head's fatal sentimentality in not killing the Flower Wine Monk outright. The moral is not "be selfish." It is "be accurate."
